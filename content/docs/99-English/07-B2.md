# A Practical 12-Week English Study Plan for B2

> **Starting point:** you already report a B2 (upper-intermediate) level. This plan strengthens fluent, accurate, independent communication across listening, speaking, reading, and writing.
>
> **Default schedule:** 30 minutes a day, six days a week, for 12 weeks—about **36 hours**. The aim is stronger B2 performance and identifying what to study next, not a guaranteed move to C1 or an exam score.

This is the overall study plan. The [Bob the Canadian plan](../06-Bob/) is an optional method for the listening and speaking sessions. **Use its exercises within this schedule; do not run both full schedules at once.** No paid course or particular exam is assumed.

## 1. What to Improve at B2

The [British Council's B2 overview](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/level/understand-your-level/b2-upper-intermediate) describes understanding the main ideas of complex texts and communicating with a degree of fluency. Your skills may be uneven: strong reading does not automatically mean equally strong spontaneous speaking.

| Skill | Training priority | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Listening | Follow an extended explanation, identify supporting details, and distinguish claims from opinions. | First-listen notes or exercise answers, checked afterward against the recording and transcript. |
| Speaking | Explain a position, compare alternatives, clarify meaning, and respond to unexpected questions. | A recorded talk and three unprepared follow-up answers. |
| Reading | Identify the argument, relevant evidence, and qualifications without translating every sentence. | A short summary in your own words, with evidence locations marked. |
| Writing | Produce clear, organized text suited to its reader and purpose. | An original draft, feedback, and a revision with reasons for the changes. |
| Vocabulary | Turn familiar but passive expressions into usable collocations and paraphrases. | Appropriate use of an expression on different days and in different contexts. |
| Grammar and pronunciation | Repair recurring problems that affect clarity, accuracy, or ease of understanding. | One corrected pattern and one short pronunciation comparison each week. |

B2 practice is not about using the longest words or speaking as fast as possible. Prioritize clear ideas, appropriate language, and the ability to recover when you do not know a word.

## 2. Keep the Resource Set Small

Start with the British Council's B2 sections as the core materials, and use Bob or another speaker for variety. Links are collected in the Reference section.

| Resource | Role in this plan | Selection rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| British Council B2 listening | Monday's focused listening, with exercises and transcript support. | Choose one manageable lesson; attempt comprehension before reading the transcript. |
| British Council B2 reading | Tuesday's reading and summary. | Choose one short text or an excerpt of roughly 400–700 words; shorter texts are fine. |
| British Council B2 speaking | Models for Wednesday/Friday discussion functions. | Pick one function, such as giving advice or disagreeing; apply it to a different situation. |
| British Council B2 writing | Models for Thursday's email, report, review, or argument. | Notice audience, organization, and tone; do not copy the model's sentences. |
| British Council B1–B2 grammar | Saturday's targeted repair. | Choose a lesson based on an error in your own output, not simply the next item in a list. |
| Bob the Canadian | Optional listening, pronunciation, and everyday-language practice. | Select a connected explanation or story; skip material that is effortless to understand and discuss. |
| A conversation partner or tutor | Friday's real interaction and occasional feedback. | Ask for follow-up questions and correction of one recurring problem, not constant interruption. |

For listening, use a **3–7-minute recording or excerpt**. Start at normal speed without captions, then check specific gaps. Choose material whose main message you can follow but which still offers useful detail or language to examine.

From Week 5, occasionally replace a familiar learning clip with a short interview, discussion, or work-related explanation from another speaker. Such material is **not necessarily CEFR-graded**; reduce its length if necessary. Understanding one teacher is not the same as following varied accents and unscripted conversation.

Select the next week's materials during Saturday planning. If browsing takes longer than expected, shorten the exercise rather than extending the session indefinitely.

## 3. Week 1: Establish a Baseline

Use the first week's normal sessions to collect a baseline—no additional study day is needed.

- **Monday:** listen once without captions. Record the main point, three details, and one cause, contrast, or qualification; verify them afterward.
- **Tuesday:** read a fresh text without a dictionary on the first pass. Write a summary and identify where the text supports it.
- **Wednesday:** record a two-minute explanation using at most five keywords, then answer three unprepared questions.
- **Thursday:** write 150–200 words for a stated reader and purpose. Save the original before checking grammar or requesting feedback.
- **Saturday:** identify one main weakness and one recurring error. Use them to guide Weeks 2–3.

Use the same preparation time and similar task types when comparing later work. These samples help select exercises; they do not certify or independently confirm your CEFR level.

## 4. The Weekly Schedule: 30 Minutes per Day

Use one broad topic across the week so that listening and reading supply ideas and language for speaking and writing. The timings below already include review and note-taking.

| Day | Focus | Time allocation | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Listening → speaking | 3 min recall; 7 min first listen and notes; 8 min targeted checking; 8 min retelling/pronunciation; 4 min log. | Checked listening notes and a short spoken summary. |
| Tuesday | Reading → summary | 3 min recall; 10 min reading; 7 min argument/evidence analysis; 8 min summary; 2 min log. | A 60–100-word summary and one question about the text. |
| Wednesday | Speaking → interaction | 3 min recall; 5 min model/prompt preparation; 8 min speaking and retry; 8 min follow-ups; 4 min replay; 2 min log. | An organized talk, three unprepared answers, and one corrected sentence. |
| Thursday | Writing | 3 min recall; 4 min outline/model structure; 18 min draft; 5 min self-check. | A 150–200-word draft with an explicit audience and purpose. |
| Friday | Conversation or transfer | With a partner: 3 min recall; 20 min discussion; 7 min feedback/notes. Solo: 3 min recall; 5 min new-speaker clip; 7 min checking; 12 min responses; 3 min log. | A discussion record or response recording, plus a clarification and a follow-up answer. |
| Saturday | Feedback, repair, and planning | 3 min recall; 10 min writing revision; 7 min grammar repair; 7 min expression review; 3 min next-week planning. | A revised paragraph or full draft, one corrected pattern, and next week's materials. |
| Sunday | Rest | No required study. Optional relaxed listening or reading. | No catch-up debt. |

Within Monday's retelling/pronunciation slot, practice a difficult **15–30-second extract**: listen, transcribe or repeat, check, then record it again. Do not add a separate long shadowing session.

### Weekly Minimum Deliverables

- One focused listening lesson and one reading text.
- One main speaking recording with three follow-up answers.
- One conversation or a second-speaker listening-and-response session.
- One 150–200-word draft, followed by a revision of its weakest part.
- About **6–10 useful expressions** reviewed and reused, plus one recurring error addressed.

These are workload guidelines, not quotas to meet at the expense of understanding. Keep the material shorter when revision takes longer.

## 5. The 12-Week Progression

The topics below are prompts for choosing suitable resources, not official lesson titles. Grammar suggestions are optional: prioritize errors actually appearing in your work.

| Week | Topic and communication goal | Speaking task | Writing task | Possible language focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Work, study, and habits: establish the baseline. | Compare two ways to organize your day and justify a preference. | An email proposing a practical change. | Reasons, examples, and frequency expressions. |
| 2 | Experiences and lessons learned. | Tell a past experience clearly and explain what you learned. | A reflective account for a colleague or friend. | Past-time sequencing and tense consistency. |
| 3 | Consumer choices and recommendations. | Compare two options using clear criteria and discuss a drawback. | A product or service review with a recommendation. | Comparatives, contrast, and useful collocations. |
| 4 | Checkpoint 1: explanation and comparison. | A fresh two-minute recommendation plus follow-up questions. | A new recommendation email under baseline conditions. | Review the most frequent error from Weeks 1–3. |
| 5 | Workplace problems and solutions. | Explain a problem, likely causes, and two possible actions. | A short problem–solution report. | Cause/effect and modals expressing uncertainty. |
| 6 | Learning and communication. | Explain an unfamiliar process to a nonexpert and check understanding. | Clear instructions or an explanatory email. | Sequencing, relative clauses, and paraphrasing. |
| 7 | Community and lifestyle choices. | Defend an opinion while acknowledging a reasonable objection. | An opinion paragraph or short article for a general audience. | Concession and qualified claims. |
| 8 | Checkpoint 2: argument and interaction. | A fresh two-minute comparison; optionally extend the discussion afterward. | A new recommendation email comparable to the baseline. | Repair problems that recur despite earlier correction. |
| 9 | Plans, risks, and alternatives. | Explain what you would do if a plan changed. | A proposal including a contingency. | Conditionals and degrees of certainty. |
| 10 | Negotiation and disagreement. | Negotiate priorities and propose a workable compromise. | A polite request, complaint, or response. | Register, tact, clarification, and indirect requests. |
| 11 | Technology and work: transfer language. | Explain a technical issue without unnecessary jargon and answer objections. | A short report for a nontechnical reader. | Concision, precise vocabulary, and audience awareness. |
| 12 | Final checkpoint and next-cycle planning. | A fresh two-minute comparison plus follow-ups; compare with Week 1. | A final recommendation email and an evidence-based revision. | Identify what now works independently and what still needs support. |

During **ordinary Wednesday practice**, aim for a two-minute main talk in Weeks 1–4, three minutes in Weeks 5–8, and up to four minutes in Weeks 9–12. Checkpoint talks stay at two minutes for a more consistent comparison. A longer talk is not automatically a better one.

Keep everyday and workplace topics mixed. Technical knowledge can make a familiar subject easier without necessarily demonstrating broader language progress.

## 6. How to Practice Each Skill

### Listening: Understand, Then Diagnose

1. Listen once at 1× without captions or a transcript. Note the message and key relationships.
2. Complete any comprehension exercise before checking its answers. Keep the first attempt separate from retries.
3. Replay only the difficult passage. Decide whether the gap was vocabulary, connected speech, attention, or background knowledge.
4. Use the transcript to verify wording, then replay with it hidden. Treat automatic captions as potentially inaccurate.
5. Summarize in your own words. Clearly separate the speaker's view from your own response.

Slowing a short extract can help diagnosis, but return to 1× afterward. Speeding up a familiar teacher is not a substitute for hearing different speakers.

### Reading: Follow the Argument

- Skim for purpose and organization, then read for the main claim, evidence, and qualifications.
- Look up only words blocking meaning or expressions you want to reuse, usually no more than three to five per text.
- Ask: “What is the writer claiming?”, “What supports it?”, and “What is limited or left uncertain?” Do not invent a hidden attitude where the text offers no evidence.
- Close the text and write the summary; reopen it to check accuracy. A string of copied sentences is not a paraphrase.

### Speaking: Generate, Do Not Recite

Use **point → reason → example → limitation/conclusion** when explaining an opinion. For narratives, use **situation → event → response → result → reflection**.

- Prepare up to five keywords rather than full sentences.
- Record the first attempt, listen for one problem, and retry with a different example.
- Practice clarification: “Do you mean ...?” and “What I mean is ...”.
- Ask a partner to challenge your reasoning or change one condition. Alone, select follow-up prompts only after your talk.
- Include questions such as “When would that not work?”, “What is the main disadvantage?”, and “How would you explain that more simply?”

Solo practice develops retrieval but does not fully reproduce interruptions, turn-taking, or negotiation. Use real conversation when available; no booking or paid service is required by this plan.

### Writing: Draft → Feedback → Revision

Before writing, specify **reader, purpose, and desired response**. Plan a clear opening, organized supporting points, and an appropriate ending.

On Thursday, check whether the draft answers the task, whether each paragraph has a purpose, and whether the tone fits the reader. On Saturday, revise the weakest paragraph first; revise the whole draft if time permits. Keep the original so that feedback does not erase evidence of your own ability.

The 150–200-word range is a practice constraint, **not an official B2 or exam requirement**. If preparing for a particular exam later, replace it with that exam's actual task format and marking criteria.

## 7. Vocabulary, Grammar, and Feedback

### Build an Active Expression Bank

Collect about 6–10 expressions from the week's input, especially ones you recognize but do not use smoothly. Review them approximately **1, 3, 7, and 14 days** later inside the scheduled recall/review slots.

| Field | What to save |
| --- | --- |
| Expression and source | The phrase, URL, and timestamp or paragraph. |
| Meaning and register | A short English explanation; note whether it is formal, neutral, or conversational. |
| Two personal examples | Use it in different situations, not just small variations of the same sentence. |
| Retrieval prompt | A situation or meaning cue that does not reveal the expression. |
| Review result | Retrieved independently, needed a hint, or still inaccurate. |

Examples of useful functions include **take something into account**, **to some extent**, **a potential drawback is ...**, and **what I mean is ...**. These are illustrative expressions, not a prescribed B2 vocabulary list.

If the review backlog grows, stop adding expressions temporarily. An expression is provisionally active when you can retrieve it on separate days and use it appropriately in more than one context.

### Repair One Repeated Error at a Time

Use Saturday's grammar slot to:

1. Find an actual repeated error in your recording or draft.
2. Check the relevant explanation and a few examples.
3. Correct your original sentence.
4. Produce three new examples with the same pattern.
5. Check whether the error recurs in the next week's spontaneous output.

Review pronunciation similarly: identify a sound, stress pattern, or word boundary that affects understanding, then compare a short before-and-after recording. The goal is intelligibility, not removing your accent.

### Get Feedback Without Outsourcing the Task

A teacher or proficient partner can check whether your meaning is clear and your phrasing natural. If using an AI tool, provide only nonconfidential content and treat corrections as suggestions to verify, not authoritative grading.

```text
Audience and purpose:
My original draft or transcript:

Please identify the three most important issues affecting clarity,
organization, grammar, or natural phrasing. For each issue:
- Quote my original wording.
- Explain the problem and suggest a minimal correction.
- Distinguish errors from optional style improvements.
- Give one short practice question.

Do not rewrite the entire response or assign a CEFR level.
I will revise it myself before asking for another check.
```

## 8. Checkpoints: Weeks 4, 8, and 12

Use the normal weekly slots, **replacing practice rather than adding a separate exam day**. Choose unseen materials before the week starts and keep length, task type, and preparation conditions similar to Week 1.

| Day / skill | Check | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday / listening | A fresh 3–7-minute recording at 1×, captions off on the first listen. | Exercise score with its denominator, or accuracy of five meaning units: main point, three details, and one relationship. Verify afterward. |
| Tuesday / reading | A fresh text of comparable length and complexity, with no dictionary on the first pass. | Comprehension exercise results and a summary checked against the source. Record any unsupported claim. |
| Wednesday / speaking | A fresh comparison/recommendation task, a two-minute talk, and three unprepared follow-ups. | Organization, relevant answers, recurring errors, and long pauses per minute. |
| Thursday / writing | A new recommendation email for a comparable reader, using the same 18-minute drafting limit and 150–200-word range. | The original draft before assistance: task coverage, organization, appropriate register, and recurring errors. |
| Friday / transfer | Keep the normal conversation or other-speaker task. | Whether performance transfers beyond the familiar source or prepared topic. |
| Saturday / review | Revise Thursday's draft, check the main error pattern, test eight older expressions, and choose the next focus. | Appropriate expression uses out of eight, revision evidence, and one concrete adjustment. |

Use these **suggested local targets**, not CEFR cutoffs:

- Listening: four of five meaning units correct, or roughly 80% on a resource's own comprehension exercise.
- Reading: a faithful summary of the main claim with two supporting points; no major unsupported conclusion.
- Speaking: a clear position, reason, example, and qualification; relevant answers to all three follow-ups.
- Writing: the reader can understand the purpose and requested action; paragraphs are connected and errors do not regularly obscure meaning.
- Vocabulary: at least six of eight sampled expressions used appropriately without looking.

Different texts and tests vary in difficulty. Record source, duration, support used, and score denominator; do not equate one good score with a higher CEFR level. For speaking and writing, seek outside feedback when possible rather than relying only on self-ratings.

If listening and reading are comfortable but speaking remains hesitant, move five minutes from input analysis to spontaneous output on the relevant day. If comprehension remains weak, shorten or simplify the material and repeat a topic week. Change one variable at a time.

## 9. Weekly Log and Busy-Day Options

```text
Week / topic:
Main bottleneck:
Listening and reading sources:
First-attempt results and support used:
Speaking recording / follow-up answers:
Writing original / feedback / revision:
Expressions active / tested:
Recurring error / correction / new examples:
Conversation or other-speaker practice:
Sessions completed out of six:
One improvement supported by evidence:
One adjustment and selected materials for next week:
```

| Available time or problem | Adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| Only 10 minutes today | Recall for 2 minutes, revisit a short familiar passage for 3, give a new response for 3, and correct one sentence for 2. Add no new material. |
| An optional extra 15 minutes | Add relaxed reading, another speaker, or conversation. Do not automatically increase the vocabulary quota. |
| Missed several days | Resume the current topic with a small task. Extend the plan instead of doubling tomorrow's workload. |
| A specialized topic is unusually difficult | Check a few background terms or choose a more familiar topic. One topic-specific gap does not invalidate your overall B2 level. |
| Progress feels stalled | Compare actual recordings and drafts. Decide whether the problem is comprehension, retrieval, accuracy, or interaction before changing resources. |

## 10. Start This Week

1. Choose one topic that matters to you and one B2 listening lesson plus one reading text.
2. Reserve six 30-minute sessions, including Friday interaction and Saturday revision.
3. Save Week 1's first attempts before corrections; they are your baseline.
4. Begin a small expression bank and correct one repeated error.
5. At the end of the week, keep what worked and adjust only the largest bottleneck.

**The learning cycle: understand → retrieve → express → get feedback → revise → reuse.**
